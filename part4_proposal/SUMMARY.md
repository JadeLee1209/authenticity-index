# Part 4 Summary — Does talk lead walk?

## Question

When a firm changes what it says it values, which way does the influence run?
- **Talk leads walk.** Practice follows: public statements act as commitments that pull behaviour
  toward them ("aspirational talk").
- **Walk leads talk.** The firm starts talking about what it already does.
- **Neither.** Talk simply tracks the public mood while practice moves on its own
  ("decoupling").

Organisation theory predicts each of these. The 2016–2024 period is a natural test, because it
contains both a surge in diversity (DEI) language after 2020 and a retreat after 2023.

## Design in one paragraph

We use the firm × theme × year panel from Part 3: 47 firms, 12 themes, up to 8 year-to-year
changes. For each pair we ask two questions:
- Does a change in what a firm *says* about a theme this year (website salience) predict a
  change in how strongly its proxy *backs* that theme next year?
- And the reverse: does a change in backing predict next year's change in talk?

Working in year-to-year changes removes everything that is constant for a firm and theme (energy
firms always stress safety, for example). Year and theme effects remove economy-wide waves, and
standard errors allow for correlation within each firm.

## Result

1. **Across all themes, neither direction shows up.** A step up in stated emphasis is not followed
   by more backing (t = 1.2), and more backing is not followed by more talk (t = −0.3). For core
   values such as integrity, customers and innovation, talk and practice move independently.
   That fits the decoupling account.

2. **Hot-button themes are the exception, and talk leads.** For diversity and climate only, a
   one-step rise in stated emphasis is followed by a rise in proxy backing the next year
   (+2.9 percentile points, t = 3.0, p ≈ 0.003). This survives a correction for the six
   regressions run. The reverse direction is not significant (t = 1.2). The effect is small:
   about 0.09 standard deviations.

   On the themes where firms were under the most public pressure, words came first and some
   practice followed. This is the "aspirational talk" pattern, but only where talk was
   scrutinised.

3. **The DEI cycle: talk peaked first, and pay was the last to move in both directions.** Each
   series below is indexed to its own peak (= 100).

   | | 2016 | 2019 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | DEI emphasis on websites | 48 | 76 | 90 | **100** | 81 | 76 |
   | Substantive DEI disclosure in proxies | 26 | 58 | **100** | 97 | 80 | 67 |
   | Share of firms paying executives on DEI metrics | 0 | 0 | 13 | 77 | **100** | 86 |

   - Proxy disclosure and website emphasis rose together.
   - Proxies filed in spring 2021 describe 2020, so they reacted to the same events as the
     mid-2021 websites.
   - DEI metrics entered executive pay only from 2021, peaked in 2023, and in 2024 were still at
     86% of their peak, while website emphasis had fallen to 76%.

4. **When DEI retreated, words and practice fell together, so we find no clear "DEI-hushing".**
   27 firms had clearly emphasised DEI on their website at some point. We dated which fell first
   after the peak: the website language, or the substantive DEI disclosure in the proxy.

   | Pattern | Proxy measured against peers | Proxy measured in absolute terms |
   |---|---|---|
   | Website language fell first ("talk first") | 14 | 12 |
   | Proxy disclosure fell first ("walk first") | 5 | 12 |
   | Both in the same year | 2 | 1 |
   | No retreat by 2024 | 6 | 2 |
   | Exact binomial test, talk-first vs walk-first | p = 0.064 | p = 1.00 |

   Measured against peers, it looks as if firms quietly dropped the words while keeping the
   practice. That impression does not survive. A peer-relative score cannot see a retreat that
   every firm makes at the same time, and in 2023–24 nearly every firm cut its DEI disclosure.
   Measured in absolute terms, talk-first and walk-first retreats are exactly balanced (12 each),
   and almost every firm retreated on both fronts.

   We report the absolute version as the result, and the peer-relative version as a cautionary
   example of how a relative index can manufacture a pattern.

   The one part of the DEI practice that clearly lagged the words is executive pay. Pay metrics
   were adopted last and were still near their peak in 2024 (point 3).

## Why it matters

- **On contested themes, words come first on the way up, but not on the way down.** In the
  retreat, talk and disclosure fell together. Only pay persisted.
- **Pay is the stickiest signal of commitment.** For an investor or regulator, incentive metrics
  are a more reliable read of whether a value is real than the language around them.
- **"Authenticity" has a time dimension.** A gap between talk and walk in one year may be a firm
  in transition rather than a firm being insincere.
- **Relative scores need an absolute cross-check.** The OAI is built from peer-relative backing.
  As the DEI retreat shows, a relative measure is blind to moves the whole market makes together,
  so time-series claims should be checked against absolute measures.

## How much weight to put on it

- **Short panel.** There are at most 8 annual changes per firm and theme, and timing is coarse:
  one website snapshot per year, and proxies that report on the prior fiscal year.
- **Relative measure.** Backing is measured relative to peers, so the regressions describe
  changes in relative position.
- **Possible bias.** The models include the outcome's own lag in changes, which can bias that
  coefficient. The coefficient of interest is much less exposed, but not immune.
- **Precedence, not causation.** That one thing comes first does not show that it caused the
  other. Other forces, such as investor pressure or litigation risk, could drive both with
  different delays.
- **Next step.** Use quarterly or monthly website snapshots around 2020 and 2023, and outcome
  data such as EEO-1 workforce reports and emissions. That would show whether practice really
  persisted behind the quieter words.
